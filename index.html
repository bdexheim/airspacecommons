Shared Airspace Commons

Brian DexheimerRuoming XinPratyusha Vellala

Drones are about to become Chicago's newest sensing network, and most of the data they collect will stay with the companies that fly them. We propose treating the low sky as one shared commons: operators pay their way in data instead of fees, city agencies read from one picture, and the people under the flights can see them, question them and say no. The Chicago Department of Public Health (CDPH) moves in first.

The problem
Why now, and why can't one agency do it alone?
1.1 · Why now

Doing more with less, before the sky fills up

Governments love to talk about increasing efficiency, but in many cases they default back to taxing more and more while services get more expensive. Chicago is in that spot right now, and drones are about to fill the sky over its most burdened blocks. How might the urban airspace actually help CDPH and other agencies build preventive capacity and do more with less?

2What CDPH needs to do better

Three cases from CDPH's recent history. Each one needed evidence CDPH didn't have when it mattered.

Environmental justice · Block Club Chicago, Jan 16, 2024

The Little Village implosion

On April 11, 2020, a smokestack implosion covered Little Village in dust. Hilco later paid a $12.25M settlement.

Instead: a block-by-block map of where the plume went within minutes, and a public record of what was measured and what CDPH did.
Extreme heat · Block Club Chicago, Dec 21, 2023

Heat Watch, one day a year

About 100 volunteers measured a 22°F spread across the city on one day in July 2023. Official readings come from two airports.

Instead: routine flights logging temperature, so the one-day map becomes a continuous record for every summer.
Inspection and equity · Borderless, Apr 2, 2025

Inspections follow complaints

With three inspectors, most inspections answer complaints, and most complaints come from higher-income areas.

Instead: the same inspectors working a list ranked by burden and verified readings, not by who calls.

The proposal
What is it?
2.1 · The commons

Built once by the city. Every relationship runs through it.

Goal

Act before harm builds

Many agencies can act before harm builds, instead of reacting once it already has. The urban airspace should help them build preventive capacity and do more with less.

Goal

Protection first, and a fair share of the sky

Equity is not just an activist trait for public agencies; the services they provide are for all. Protection reaches the most burdened blocks first, and no block carries more than its share of the sky.

Goal

Answerable to the people below

Public agencies provide services to the public, but too often do not truly answer to them. Every reading should be answerable to the people beneath it.

Left: the three archetypes as overlapping rings; take any one away and the other two come apart. Right: what moves between the city base and each relationship. DTI runs the base; it decides nothing about who may read what.

Where the rings overlap

1 and 2

One pod serves several agencies, so added readers don't add flights. Operators face one menu with a cap on total asks, not five departments.

One cap per block, counted across every agency, and every flight visible to the people under it. Panels can ask for a lower share.

2 and 3

Every reading remembers why it was taken. No new agency or use joins without the purpose rules, the panels and a public vote.

The choices underneath

Operators pay in data, not money

Use a city launch site, share a basic flight record. Share more, get better weather and route information back.

Every reading remembers why it was taken

Health data stays health data. Adding a new use takes a public decision.

"Fair" has two sides

A cap on how much any block carries, counted across every agency, and each agency's own promise about who it serves first.

Residents help define burden

Built on Chicago's own environmental justice map, which residents helped make. Neighborhood panels say yes or no to cameras.

The one who runs it doesn't decide on it

The city's tech office runs the platform. An independent board with resident seats holds the data.

Most of the time, nobody has to referee

Once data exists it can be shared. Only the flights themselves are scarce, and that mostly matters in emergencies.

4.3 · Six conditions

What has to be true: six conditions

The commons holds only as far as the city's ground lever reaches, and only if the burden budget really caps total flying. The city can enforce a cap only through the ground it controls: launch sites, hubs and permits.

Everything rests on this

A burden budget the city can enforce

Tied to siting, launch-site access and permits, counted across every agency's flights, never loosened as the industry grows.

An owner that isn't a tenant

DTI hosts the platform. An independent custodian board, created by ordinance, decides who may use the data.

Purpose rules with teeth

A closed list of purposes, a public vote to add one, a never-allowed list, rules on which carriers are eligible, and data minimized at the source.

A named arbiter

OEMC in emergencies. For everyday conflicts: the protected burden share first, then purpose, then first come.

A cost split

The base is funded centrally, add-ons are paid by whoever asks, and regulated facilities pay for their own impacts.

Paid participation

Stipends for crews and panel members, and a seating rule that puts the highest-burden blocks first.

Footing the city already has

  • Permits can already require monitoring: CDPH requires facility-run continuous dust monitors (2020–21). Airborne monitoring would extend that.
  • The 2021 air quality siting review covers warehousing and freight uses, which would likely include a delivery hub.
  • The city asks operators only for what Remote ID doesn't already broadcast: stored logs, planned routes, operator purpose and noise class.

Still open

  • The airport ceiling over a burdened edge. The blocks CDPH most needs to read sit where the airport wants the fewest flights. No moment settles it yet, so ground sensors have to stand in.
  • The rules feed. Whether the city publishes its own, or works through the services operators already use.
  • Thin areas. Whether the burden cap reduces service in areas that are thinly served already.

Is it worth it?
How does it grow, and what does it cost?
5.1 · How it grows

Three steps, each one earned

The commons doesn't start whole. It grows in the airspace in three steps, and each one has to pass a gate before the next begins. The rules that make it answerable (purpose labels, the ledger, the custodian board) are there from the first day.

1 · RIDE-ALONG

Passive data only

Base 1 at new hubs, the base map, CDPH and Water Management reading, the ledger live from day one.

Gate: a full season with no purpose breach, panels seated in the highest-burden neighborhoods.

2 · STEERED

Add-ons and the gap queue

Shared pods, the add-on menu with its cap, a protected share for thin blocks. CDOT, CFD, OEMC and an ecology steward join.

Gate: OEMC agrees to arbitrate, agencies sign a cost split, the burden budget is measured for a season.

3 · HELD

The budget enforced

The burden budget caps every flight, the city has an emergency mode, and panels can ask for a lower share.

Then: only once this is stable does the pattern try another shared space.

5.2 · What it costs

More capacity, without a much bigger budget

This is the mock budget for the Council audience. The question isn't "how much does the commons cost?" It's "what would it cost to get the same capacity the usual way, by hiring more people, buying more sensors and running more flights?" The numbers are rough estimates sized from what we found. The point is the comparison, not the decimals.

1 · The cliff

The sensor grant ran out

Open Air Chicago's 277 sensors launched on more than $4M, and the $2M federal grant behind them ended in September. CDPH has three air inspectors and a shrinking budget.

2 · What's missing

Readings where the harm is

Fixed sensors can't follow a dust plume down a fence line. Inspections still mostly start with a complaint, and the most burdened blocks often complain least.

3 · The usual way

Hire, buy and fly it yourself

More inspectors, a city drone fleet with its own pilots, and every department contracting its own flights. It works, but each piece is paid for separately.

4 · The commons way

Ride on flights already happening

City pods ride on delivery drones, operators earn credits instead of cash, paid resident crews check the readings, and every department reads from one platform.

5 · What it doesn't fix

It's not a deficit fix

It doesn't close the city's budget gap or replace inspectors. It buys capacity the city can't afford the usual way.

Where the new money comes from

SourceHowPer year

Facilities near the sensors pay for their own monitoring under their permits. That cost sits with them, outside the city budget, and their data is one of the checks.

What this budget doesn't do

  • It doesn't touch the $882M gap projected for 2027. It's a small line that buys capacity the city can't afford the usual way.
  • It doesn't replace inspectors. CDPH still has three; the commons sends them where burden is highest, and paid crews extend their reach.
  • The savings depend on operators flying here. If hubs move outside the city line, the anchor sensors and crews carry the load alone, and the usual-way costs come back.
  • One budget line is one target. If it's cut, every department loses its view of the sky at once.
How we sized these numbers

Every figure here is a rough estimate for a class project, sized from public sources:

  • Inspectors. CDPH's Senior Environmental Inspector posting starts at $73,140 (City of Chicago posting, Aug 2025). We used about $100K a year with benefits.
  • Sensors. Professional-grade low-cost sensors run about $500–$5,000 per unit-year all-in; reference monitors cost $15,000–$40,000 plus more than $15,000 a year to run (Clarity pricing guide, a vendor source).
  • Flights. A worked example puts an in-house drone program at about $114 per flight hour for aircraft, sensors and overhead, plus pilot time (Commercial UAV News, Oct 2026). Docked fleets bring that down, which is why we sized the city fleet from Cincinnati's numbers instead.
  • Benefits and pensions. Chicago's 2025 budget put $2.9B toward pensions and $758M toward employee health and insurance, across about 36,000 budgeted positions (Civic Federation, Oct 2025). Much of the pension cost pays for past service, so we loaded new city staff at about 1.6 times salary: roughly $120K a year for an inspector.
  • A city drone fleet. Cincinnati's 22 docked police drones cost about $62,000 each with dock, training and warranty, plus about $450,000 a year in software and maintenance (NationGraph, Feb 2026). Routine sensing over Chicago's burdened blocks would need about twice that fleet, plus air sensors, remote operators, rooftop sites, insurance and beyond-line-of-sight approvals. We used about $3.5M a year, with hardware replaced every four years.
  • What the city already spends or owes. Keeping the sensor network running after the grant, plus the flight and inspection contracts departments already pay for. These are our assumptions, not city figures.

How we got here
What method got us here?
7.1 · The method

From one agency's tool to a shared commons

We started with a detailed picture of how CDPH could use the airspace, and four bundles built around it. But when we abstracted it, we only swapped CDPH for "your agency." The system underneath stayed the same, insinuating duplicated effort and no coordination between agencies.

So we asked a different question: is CDPH really the only public agency that would benefit from the urban airspace as a sensing network? It isn't, and that turned the project from one agency's tool into the shared commons in chapter 2. The steps below are the course method we followed, the Strategic Choice-Making Model.

01 · PROMPTING

A moment and a claim

We bounded the problem at the inspector's morning list. From its position as advocate and anchor tenant, CDPH reshapes how Chicago gathers airspace data and shares the capacity to collect it.

02 · PROTOTYPING

18 prototypes, 2 shared bases

We rebuilt the set so CDPH's shareable tools became city infrastructure that any agency can use.

03 · ARCHETYPING

Three archetypes

One for each relationship: operators and the city, agency to agency, the city and its residents.

04 · INFRASTRUCTURING

Telling it, showing it

Six action situations, four audiences, and the case for building it.

Credits, and how we used AI

Frameworks

  • The Strategic Choice-Making Model (SCMM). From Deaa Bataineh's dissertation, The Craft of Strategic Choice-Making, adapted as the Complex Systems Innovation course instrument at Illinois Tech. Its four stages, Prompting, Prototyping, Archetyping and Infrastructuring, structure this whole project.
  • Anatomy of Infrastructures (AOI). A course framework: the rings of features, affordances, impacts and goals in chapter 4.
  • Action situations. A course method, after Elinor Ostrom's Institutional Analysis and Development (IAD) framework: one bounded moment with its participants, information and outcomes.
  • The brief. The UAC Design Brief and its systemic snapshots (course material).

The D-Droid, and what it did

We worked through every stage of the SCMM with the D-Droid, the course's AI agents configured with the SCMM source code. It questioned our framing, held each stage's completion conditions and argued against our favorite concepts. We also used the D-Droid to generate the scene illustrations; the team decided what each scene shows, placed the numbered markers and wrote the annotations and captions.

The choices, interpretations and claims are ours. Facts about Chicago today link to their sources below.
