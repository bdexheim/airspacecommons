Council and budget · one-page briefMock material

Build it once: one sensing base for the city's low sky

For
Council members and budget staff
From
CDPH, with DTI
Decision
Fund the base; seat a custodian board
The ask
  1. Fund the city base centrally: about $0.9M a year at citywide scale, from the corporate fund.
  2. Create an independent custodian board by ordinance to decide who may use the data.

Why now

  • The sensor grant ran out. Open Air Chicago's 277 sensors launched on more than $4M; the $2M federal grant behind them ended in September. CDPH has three air inspectors.
  • Drones are arriving anyway. Delivery service is starting from Markham and Matteson, and the FAA's rule for routine long-range flights is in final review.
  • The rules get written after the fact. Drones are showing up with police first. We want the rules for who reads the data written first.

What it buys, year 3, citywide

Sensing hours a year over burdened blocks12,000
Readings a year from paid resident crews3,600
Days from a high reading to an inspection3 or fewer
Departments reading one shared picture5
Blocks with a monthly flight and air recordEvery block

Today: none of these, and inspections wait weeks, if someone complains.

What it costs

The usual way$7.9Ma year · $5.4M newMore inspectors, a city drone fleet, each department contracting its own flights.
The commons way$4.5Ma year · $2.0M newCity pods ride on flights already happening; operators earn credits, not cash.
Where the $2.0M comes from
City corporate fund (the base)$0.9M
Department shares$0.5M
State and federal grants$0.3M
Credits instead of permit fees$0.3M

Both columns buy the same capacity; the commons does it for about 43% less. One year of children's asthma ER visits in Chicago was charged $18.7M. Preventing about one in nine of them would roughly match the new cost.

What it won't do

  • It doesn't touch the $882M gap projected for 2027.
  • It doesn't replace inspectors; it sends them where burden is highest.
  • It depends on operators flying here. If hubs move outside the city line, the usual costs come back.

What it costs you

One budget line is one target: if it's cut, every department loses its view of the sky at once. And one breach exposes every agency, which is why the custodian board sits outside any one of them.

Other agencies · onboarding kitMock material

Joining the commons: what you keep, what you share

See more of the sky for less, and keep your own layer, thresholds and playbooks. CDPH plugs in first; every agency after it joins on the same terms.

You keep

  • Your own data layer, and the purposes it serves
  • Your thresholds: which reading triggers action, and how it's confirmed
  • Your response playbooks

You share

  • A share of the base cost, instead of growing your own program
  • Your purposes, registered on one closed list
  • One tasking queue for flights
  • Joint exercises

You get

  • The base map
  • Short loops on flights already happening, through the queue
  • One backup plan for the day drones are grounded
  • A named arbiter

How conflicts are settled

EmergenciesOEMC decides
Everyday conflictsThe block's protected share of the sky first, then purpose, then first come

What it costs you

Some discretion goes to the arbiter and to an access matrix. Your layer can be used only for the purposes you registered, and a new purpose needs a public vote by the neighborhood panel.

Joining, in four steps

  1. Register your purposes. Each one goes on the closed list. Anything new goes to the neighborhood panel first.
  2. Put your layer and thresholds on the base map. The map, the payload standard and the queue are already built.
  3. Agree your cost share. It replaces flights you would otherwise contract yourself.
  4. Exercise together, including a day when every drone is grounded and ground sensors carry the load.
Never allowed, whatever the vote Health readings used for enforcement against residents. Cameras over homes.

Operators · one-page briefMock material

One front door: flying from a city site

One front door, one rules feed, and a pooled weather and route picture, for data you already log. You pay in data, never in cash.

Level 1 · Basic · required

Use a city site, share a basic record

You give
Stored flight logs, planned routes and the noise class of your aircraft
You get
Access to city sites and hubs, and one front door for every department
Level 2 · Trade · optional

Share more, see more

You give
Wind, temperature and humidity your aircraft already take; obstacles and conditions met en route
You get
The pooled weather picture, block by block; corridor and closure updates before you plan
Level 3 · Extras · one at a time, capped

Carry a city pod or take a short loop

You give
A city sensor pod, clipped on at the battery swap, or a short detour over the blocks the city names
You get
Credits for usable coverage, spent on better renewal terms, priority pad slots or permit-fee offsets

What the city never asks

  • Cash, at any level
  • Customer names or delivery addresses
  • Camera flights over homes, unless a neighborhood panel has said yes
  • Anything Remote ID already broadcasts
  • More than one request at a time from the same department

Where the readings go

Straight to the department, labeled with their purpose. You get a coverage receipt (blocks, minutes, quality), never the readings themselves.

When a block nears its share

Each block has a seasonal allowance of low flights. Past the review line, at 90%, new extras pause for city review and planners route around the block where the detour is short. Regular deliveries keep flying.

Who handles it, and how often

Every flightYour software checks the city's terms feed. Nothing for a person to do.
Each extra requestYour dispatch agent accepts or declines, but only within preferences a person signed.
Every monthA statement: coverage given, credits earned and spent, blocks near their line.
Once a yearA person signs the terms. Anything new goes to a person first; if the feed and the signed clause differ, the signed version wins.
What it costs you Every ask spends some goodwill, and a capped block can mean a longer route.

Residents · a short guideMock material

Your sky, your say

What flies over your block and why, how to ask for sensing, and how to decide what your sky is used for.

What's flying

Every low flight over your block is counted, whatever agency or company flew it. Your block has a seasonal share of the sky, and camera flights count double against it.

Your monthly card

Every household gets one, renters included, in the languages spoken on your block. The same numbers are in the public ledger.

Four things you can do

Look up any flight

Who flew, when, for whom and why. Scan the code on your card or visit the ledger.

Ask for sensing

Worried about something? Request a reading for your block. Requests from burdened blocks go first.

Talk to your panel

Cameras over your area need your neighborhood panel's yes, and every yes has limits and an end date. Anyone can watch and speak before a vote.

Challenge it

Something look wrong? Every challenge gets an answer.

Get paid to take part

Join a sensing crew. Carry a calibrated sensor on set routes near permitted facilities. Once your reading is checked against the city's own sensors, it moves the inspection list, and CDPH owes you an answer by a set date.

Sit on your panel. Panel seats are paid, and the blocks carrying the most burden are seated first.

What it can cost

Your panel can say no. A refusal is binding, but it can also leave a harm unmeasured. Ground sensors keep reading either way.

Never used for Enforcement against you or your neighbors. Cameras over homes.
